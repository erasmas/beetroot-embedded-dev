Getting CAN‑H and CAN‑L from the OBD2 plug into the transceiver

Six wires total. Two go to the bike, four go to the ESP32. The only part that can actually bite you is knowing which of the sixteen holes is pin 6 — so that comes first.

The confusing part

Finding pin 6 and pin 14

The OBD2 connector is a trapezoid with the long edge up: pins 1–8 across the top row, 9–16 across the bottom, both numbered left to right. Pin 6 is sixth along the top and pin 14 is sixth along the bottom, which means they sit directly above and below each other. Once you know that, they're easy to find by eye.

The trap is orientation. Looking into the adapter's female socket, pin 1 is top-left. Looking at your male plug's front face, everything mirrors and pin 1 is top-right. But you never solder from the front face — you solder from the back, and from the back the numbering lines up with the socket again.

Do not trust wire colour Cheap Honda→OBD2 adapters use whatever wire was on the reel. Colour tells you nothing about which pin a conductor lands on. Ring it out with a multimeter or go by pad position.

The whole circuit

Six wires, end to end

Two wires leave the OBD2 plug and arrive at the transceiver's CAN side. Four more leave the transceiver's logic side for the ESP32. One jumper stays on the module itself.

Remove the onboard 120 Ω before you connect to the bike Nearly every SN65HVD230 breakout carries a 120 Ω terminator across CANH–CANL, usually marked 121 or 1200. The bike's bus is already terminated at both ends — that's the ~60 Ω you measure in Step 1. Adding a third terminator drops the bus to 40 Ω, which pushes the driver harder and can produce error frames. Desolder the resistor, then confirm the module reads open between CANH and CANL on its own.

Before power

Proving you soldered the right two pads

You don't have to trust the drawing. With the ignition off and the pigtail mated into the adapter, the bike's own pair of terminating resistors gives you a signature you can measure from the far end of your two wires.

Resistance can't tell CAN-H from CAN-L — they're symmetric. To tell them apart, switch to DC volts with the ignition on and the bus active, referenced to chassis ground: CAN-H idles slightly above 2.5 V, CAN-L slightly below. If you get it backwards, nothing is harmed; you just see no valid frames, and swapping the two wires fixes it.

Order of operations

Doing the soldering

  1. Desolder the 120 Ω terminator from the SN65HVD230 module first, while the board is still bare and easy to hold. Verify open circuit between CANH and CANL.
  2. Cut ~25 cm of 22–24 AWG twisted pair. If you don't have pair stock, twist two separate cores together in a drill chuck — it takes a minute and it's what keeps ignition noise out of a differential line.
  3. Tin the two OBD2 pads, tin the wire ends, then join them. Pads 6 and 14 only — nothing on pin 16. Sleeve each joint in heatshrink, then a larger piece over both for strain relief where the pair exits the connector shell.
  4. Solder the far ends to CANH and CANL on the module. Keep this run short; a long stub off a terminated bus causes reflections.
  5. Run the four logic wires to the ESP32 and set RS. Header pins and Dupont jumpers are fine on the bench, but solder them before the bike sees vibration.
  6. Mate everything and take the 60 Ω reading. Only then apply power.
First power-on Configure TWAI at 500 kbps, extended IDs, accept-all filter, listen-only before the transceiver is ever connected to a live bus. In listen-only the peripheral cannot acknowledge or transmit, so a misconfiguration can't disturb the ECU — that guarantee is worth having in place from the very first boot.
